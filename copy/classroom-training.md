## Who this is for

### You want CNC skills you can actually use on Monday morning.

You're in your 20s, 30s, 40s. You might already be on a CNC, stuck at operator level and wanting to move into programming. You might be a manual machinist, a fabricator, a setter on a different process, looking to break into CNC properly. Or you've finished an apprenticeship and the formal training stopped before the interesting bit started.

I also get companies sending one or two people on a scheduled course rather than booking me onsite. That's usually because the team is small, the budget is tight, or the operator needs lifting from beginner to setter and a structured course fits better than me arriving for two days.

Either way, classroom training works the same way. Small group, real simulators, the same teaching approach I use onsite: no fixed agenda, no syllabus you could've downloaded for free, no PowerPoint. You learn by doing.

## The courses

### Eight courses across milling, turning, programming and macro.

Each course runs as a scheduled date or on-demand if you've got a group of two or more. Application is per course — pick the one that matches where you are, apply online, dates come back inside a working day.

- **Beginners — Milling.** For people new to CNC milling. Reading a programme, setting tools, work offsets, basic G-code. By the end you can set up a job from scratch and run it
- **Beginners — Turning.** Same shape as the milling beginners course, on lathe simulators. Covers tool turret setup, work offsets, basic turning cycles, common G-codes for lathes
- **Setter / Operator — Milling.** For operators who can press cycle-start but want to set the machine themselves. Tool length offsets, work coordinate systems, edge-finding, first-off inspection, dealing with a crash without panicking
- **Setter / Operator — Turning.** Lathe equivalent. Setting the turret, work offsets, dealing with bar feed, common turning problems on the shop floor
- **Learn to Program — Milling.** Goes beyond running someone else's programme. Writing G-code by hand for milling work, canned cycles, cutter compensation, sub-routines. You leave able to write a programme from a drawing
- **Learn to Program — Turning.** Lathe programming from scratch. Roughing and finishing cycles, threading, grooving, parting. You finish able to programme a typical turned part hand-coded
- **Macro Programming.** For programmers who want to move past basic G-code into parametric programming. Macro variables, IF / THEN / GOTO logic, custom canned cycles, talking to the control properly. The course that separates programmers from people who copy programmes
- **Edgecam.** CAD/CAM training in Edgecam, using my professional licence in the classroom. Cover the workflow from importing a model to posting tested G-code. Suits programmers moving from hand-code to CAM, or companies adopting Edgecam in-house

## What to expect

### Six simulators. One to six of you. Two days, three days, or a week.

The room is set up around six CNC machine simulators — real controls, real programming, real cycle-stops when something's wrong. You're not watching a screen. You're at a station, programming, running, breaking and fixing.

- **Group size:** 1 to 6 students. If only one person applies, the course still runs — you just get a much more intensive week
- **Length:** most courses run two or three days. Macro programming and Edgecam usually run longer
- **Format:** I teach, you do. Worked examples, then your own attempt, then the next problem. No long lectures. Questions get answered when they come up
- **Pace:** I adapt to the room. If a course is moving fast, I cover more ground. If something's not clicking, I slow down and stay with it until it does
- **Certificate:** issued on completion, per student. Useful for your CV, your employer's records, and your own evidence of competence
- **Where:** the classroom is in Barwell, Leicestershire. Postcode and directions sent on application

## Who shouldn't book this

### Be honest about where you're starting from.

Classroom training is the right call when you want a structured course, you can travel to Barwell, and you've got the time to apply what you learn afterwards. It's the wrong call in a few cases and I'd rather say so up front.

- **You want a degree.** This is practical training, not academic. The certificate evidences competence on the course content. It doesn't replace an HNC, an engineering degree, or a formal apprenticeship
- **You can't travel to Barwell.** Online courses are coming but aren't live yet. If you're outside the UK or can't make the trip, the Learning Hub is the best free starting point until self-paced courses launch
- **You're a complete beginner who hasn't seen a CNC.** The beginners courses assume you've at least been near a machine and understand the basics of metal cutting. If you're truly starting from zero, the Hub has months of reading that'll get you to a useful starting point
- **You're sending five people from one company.** At that group size, onsite training on your own machines is almost always the better economic and practical choice. Call me and I'll scope it that way instead

## FAQ

### The questions I get most often.

**Will this course get me a job?**
Honest answer: a certificate alone won't. A certificate plus the skills you'll actually use on the floor, plus the willingness to start somewhere and learn, will. I've had students go from a beginners course to an operator job within a month. I've had others struggle because they wanted the qualification more than they wanted the work. The course is part of the answer, not the whole answer.

**Which course do I need?**
If you've never run a CNC, beginners. If you can press cycle-start but can't set the machine, setter/operator. If you can set it but you've never written a programme from scratch, Learn to Program. If you're an experienced programmer who's never touched parametric code, macro programming. If you're not sure, call me — five minutes on the phone usually settles it.

**How many people are on each course?**
One to six. The course runs even with one applicant — you just get a much more intensive week of one-to-one time.

**Can my employer pay for me?**
Yes, lots do. I'll invoice the company direct, or you can pay and reclaim. Either works. Happy to talk to your manager if it helps the conversation.

**Are the simulators the same as real machines?**
The simulators run real controls — same Fanuc, Heidenhain, Siemens, Mazak interfaces you'll see on the shop floor. The G-code you write in the classroom runs on a real machine without rewriting. The only thing missing is the swarf.

**Do you offer accommodation?**
No, but Barwell has half a dozen hotels and B&Bs within ten minutes. I'll send a few recommendations with the booking confirmation.

**Is there a weekend option?**
Some courses have weekend variants. Check the individual course page or ask on the application form.
