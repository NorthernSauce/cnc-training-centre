## Manual machines first. Then turning. Then mills. Then everything.

<!-- H2: Bucket E broetry narrative opener. Captures the career arc in the heading itself. Distinct from any other H on the site. -->

<!-- Body para 1: VOC from existing about copy verbatim. -->
I started in CNC the way most people did in the early nineties. Running machines, picking up the next control when the next machine came in, getting handed problems nobody else could solve. Manual first. Then turning. Then mills. Then programming. Then macro. Then everything Fanuc could throw at you, and Heidenhain, and Siemens, and Mazak. Sliding head when sliding head started turning up on UK floors.

<!-- Body para 2: VOC from existing about copy. Em dash killed ("independently - because" became "independently. Because"). -->
By the mid-nineties I was the bloke other engineers in the workshop came to when something on the machine didn't make sense. That's a useful thing to be, but it pays the same as the people not solving anything. So I started training. Then I started training independently. Because the OEM gigs would only ever let me teach their own brand, and I wanted to teach the actual machine in front of the actual operator, whatever logo was on it.

<!-- Body para 3: VOC from existing about copy. -->
Thirty years on, that's still the only thing I do. Onsite at your workshop, classroom in Leicestershire, programming services when you've got the job and not the time. Same bloke on the call as the bloke on your machine. There's no team. It's me.

## One brand isn't your problem. Your problem is a mixed shop.

<!-- H2: Bucket A Problem hook (contrast pattern). VOC: existing about copy verbatim. Strong - keep. -->

<!-- Body para 1: VOC from existing about copy. -->
Most of the engineering companies I walk into don't have one machine. They have five, six, ten. Different brands, different controls, different vintages. The Mazak from 2008 next to the Hurco from last year next to the sliding head that nobody's touched in three months because the bloke who knew it left.

<!-- Body para 2: VOC from existing about copy. -->
OEM training is brand-locked by design. Mazak will train you on Mazak. Fanuc will train you on Fanuc. Both are good at their own kit. Neither will help you with the other four machines on your floor. And both will charge you per person, per day, with the syllabus they've decided you need.

<!-- Body para 3: VOC from existing about copy. -->
That's the gap I sit in. I've trained on every major control, so when I walk in I'm useful from the first machine to the last. I don't work to a syllabus. I work to what your operators need, on the kit you actually own. And the day rate is for the whole team, not per head, so training four people doesn't cost four times what training one would.

The OEMs do good work in their lane. I do good work across every lane.

## No fixed agenda. I assess as I go.

<!-- H2: Bucket E broetry. Contrarian position as hook. "No fixed agenda" verbatim from §8 phrases-to-use. -->

<!-- Body para 1: VOC from existing about copy. -->
The training works because there's no script. Some people pick something up in twenty minutes. Some take half a day. A syllabus says "by 11am we'll cover X" which means either you race ahead of half the room or you wait for the slowest. Neither is training. That's a presentation.

<!-- Body para 2: VOC from existing about copy. -->
What I do instead: walk round the shop on the first morning with whoever runs production, see what the machines are doing, see what the team's level actually is, then start where they actually are. If they're flying, I push further. If they're stuck on something basic, we stay on it until it clicks. The day rate is fixed. The scope flexes to fit the people in front of me.

<!-- Body para 3: VOC from existing about copy. -->
The other thing that matters: training on your actual machines, with your actual parts. Simulators are fine for the classroom (I run six of them) but the strongest retention comes from learning the skill the day before you apply it. Train on Monday, run the part on Tuesday. That's how it sticks.

<!-- Body para 4: VOC from existing about. -->
If you've sat through a training course before and forgotten most of it by Friday, that's why. The training wasn't connected to anything you were going to do next.

## What gets asked about me specifically?

<!-- H2: Formula B from Ch. 10 (curiosity gap). Distinct from service-page FAQ Hs. -->

**Who have you trained for?**
A mix. SMB engineering companies from five to fifty staff is the bread and butter. Larger production facilities when the OEM training calendar won't flex. Individuals paying out of their own pocket to move from operator to programmer. The OEMs themselves on the occasional referral. The common thread is people who'd rather have a bloke who's actually run the kit than a bloke who's been reading a manual.

**What machines have you owned?**
Plenty over thirty years. Currently I run six CNC simulators in the classroom covering milling, turning, and the major controls. I've trained on far more than I've owned. That's the nature of being independent. If you're curious about a specific machine, ask.

**Are you available for consulting work?**
Yes. Onsite training is the main thing, but I also take consulting engagements. Productivity audits, process work, programming efficiency, training audits where someone else has tried to lift a team and it hasn't stuck. Same day rate as onsite training. Contact me with the specifics.

**Do you only do CNC, or other engineering training too?**
CNC. That's it. Thirty years on the controls is the whole offer. If you need press-tool training or fabrication training, I'll point you to someone who does it properly.

**Why is there no team?**
Because I'm the bloke who turns up on every job. There's no junior trainer to hand the engagement off to, no associate I subcontract to, no "we" hiding two people who've never met. When you book me, you get me. That's a constraint on capacity. I'm typically booking four to six weeks out. But it's the whole point.

**Will the swearing be in the training too?**
Probably some. I keep it tighter than the articles when I'm on a shop floor with strangers. I'm not going to suddenly stop being a bloke from Leicestershire. If that's a problem, say so on the enquiry call and we'll work it out.
