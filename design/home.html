The way it actually happened

Your operators were never trained.
They were shown.

Think about how your shop floor actually learned to run those machines. One bloke got handed a manual nobody had opened since 2014. Another picked up Fanuc from a colleague who left three years ago. A new starter watched ten minutes of a YouTube clip between jobs. Nobody planned it that way. It's just what happens when the days are full and proper training is the thing that slides off the bottom of the list.

You priced the alternatives. The OEMs quoted double what you'd expect, locked to their kit only, on a syllabus built around somebody else's parts. Free online content is bottomless and unsorted, and there's nobody to ask when the video doesn't match the post-processor in front of you. So another quarter goes by. The same machine keeps running slow.

It isn't your operators failing. It's that they were trained ad-hoc, by whoever happened to have ten spare minutes that morning, who was himself trained the same way fifteen years ago.

Nothing got taught properly. The gaps compound. The machine never gets used near what it's capable of. Fix that, the rest follows.

What I do

The independent CNC trainer for British engineering shops with mixed machines and no time.

OEM trainers cover their own kit. University courses produce graduates who've never set a tool offset under pressure. YouTube doesn't show up when the spindle won't move. I do one thing, and I've done it for thirty years: walk into a workshop with mixed controls, sit down with the people running them, and leave the floor measurably faster than I found it. Every major control. Every level, beginner to programmer. Onsite on your machines, or in the classroom on simulators if you'd rather your people came to me.

The OEMs send referrals because they know I cover what their syllabus won't. The 8,930 people on my YouTube channel watch me work through real problems on real machines. None of that proves I'm good on its own. It proves I've been doing this in public, on the record, long enough that there's a paper trail.

How it works

Training that happens while the machine is running.

Most training pulls your team off the floor, sits them in a room with a PowerPoint, hands them a folder, and sends them back two days later to do the job they couldn't do before. The kit didn't run all week. The customer's still waiting. Half of what they learned won't transfer because they never touched the machine.

I do it the other way round. I come to your shop. We work on your machines, with your parts, against your real jobs. The training is the production. Your operator learns the cycle they're going to run on Monday, not a textbook example. The setter watches the offsets get dialled in on the part that's due Thursday. By the time I leave, the job is in spec and the person who couldn't do it can.

Note

The training is the production.

I assess as I go. If your operators pick something up fast, we move on. If they're struggling, I stay until it clicks. No fixed agenda, because every shop floor is a different fixed agenda already. Yours.
