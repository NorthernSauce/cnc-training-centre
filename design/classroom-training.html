Who this is for

If you've finished your apprenticeship and the interesting stuff stopped.

You're in your 20s, 30s, 40s. Already on a CNC, stuck at operator level and wanting to move into programming. Or a manual machinist, fabricator, setter on a different process, looking to break into CNC properly. Or finished an apprenticeship that stopped before the programming bit started.

I also get companies sending one or two people on a scheduled course rather than booking me onsite. Usually because the team is small, the budget is tight, or the operator needs lifting from beginner to setter and a structured course fits better than me arriving for two days.

Either way, the classroom works the same way. Small group, real simulators, the same teaching approach I use onsite. No fixed agenda, no syllabus you could've downloaded for free, no PowerPoint. You learn by doing.

After the course

What does your CV look like three months from now?

You walk in on day one of the new job and you're not bluffing. You know what offsets are. You know what to do when the cycle stops. You're not the person everyone wishes had been trained properly before they got handed the keys.

Who shouldn't book this

If any of these are you, save your £300 and don't book.

Classroom training is the right call when you want a structured course, you can travel to Barwell, and you've got the time to apply what you learn afterwards. It's the wrong call in a few cases and I'd rather say so up front.

  1. 01

    You want a degree.

    Practical training, not academic. The certificate evidences competence on the course content. Doesn't replace an HNC, an engineering degree, or a formal apprenticeship.

  2. 02

    You can't travel to Barwell.

    Online courses are coming but aren't live yet. The Learning Hub is the best free starting point until self-paced courses launch.

  3. 03

    You're a complete beginner who hasn't seen a CNC.

    The beginners courses assume you've at least been near a machine and understand the basics of metal cutting. If you're starting from zero, the Hub has months of reading that'll get you to a useful starting point.

  4. 04

    You're sending five people from one company.

    At that group size, onsite training on your own machines is almost always the better economic and practical choice. Call me. I'll scope it that way instead.

FAQ

What gets asked before someone applies?

01 Will this course get me a job?

Honest answer: a certificate alone won't. A certificate plus the skills you'll actually use on the floor, plus the willingness to start somewhere and learn, will. I've had students go from a beginners course to an operator job within a month. I've had others struggle because they wanted the qualification more than they wanted the work. The course is part of the answer, not the whole answer.

02 Which course do I need?

If you've never run a CNC, beginners. If you can press cycle-start but can't set the machine, setter/operator. If you can set it but you've never written a programme from scratch, Learn to Program. If you're an experienced programmer who's never touched parametric code, macro programming. If you're not sure, call me. Five minutes on the phone usually settles it.

03 How many people are on each course?

One to six. The course runs even with one applicant. You just get a much more intensive week of one-to-one time.

04 Can my employer pay for me?

Yes, lots do. I'll invoice the company direct, or you can pay and reclaim. Either works. Happy to talk to your manager if it helps the conversation.

05 Are the simulators the same as real machines?

The simulators run real controls. Same Fanuc, Heidenhain, Siemens, Mazak interfaces you'll see on the shop floor. The G-code you write in the classroom runs on a real machine without rewriting. The only thing missing is the swarf.

06 Do you offer accommodation?

No. Barwell has half a dozen hotels and B&Bs within ten minutes. I'll send a few recommendations with the booking confirmation.
